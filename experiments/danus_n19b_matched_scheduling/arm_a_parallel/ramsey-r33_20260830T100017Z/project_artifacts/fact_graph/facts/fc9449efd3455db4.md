## statement
Prove that every red-blue coloring of the edges of the complete graph on six vertices contains a monochromatic triangle, while there is a red-blue coloring of the complete graph on five vertices containing no monochromatic triangle. Equivalently, prove that the Ramsey number \(R(3,3)\) equals \(6\).

## proof
We prove the two assertions separately.

For the six-vertex assertion, fix an arbitrary vertex \(v\). There are exactly five other vertices, hence exactly five edges incident with \(v\). At least three of these five edges have one common color: otherwise at most two incident edges would be red and at most two would be blue, so there would be at most four incident edges, contradicting that there are five. Denote a color occurring at least three times by \(\gamma\), and choose three distinct vertices \(x,y,z\) such that the three edges from \(v\) to \(x,y,z\) all have color \(\gamma\).

Consider the three edges joining pairs of \(x,y,z\). If at least one of these edges has color \(\gamma\), then its two endpoints together with \(v\) form a triangle all three of whose edges have color \(\gamma\). If none of these three edges has color \(\gamma\), then, because the only two colors are red and blue, all three edges joining pairs of \(x,y,z\) have the color different from \(\gamma\). In that case \(x,y,z\) themselves form a monochromatic triangle. These two cases are exhaustive, so every red-blue coloring on six vertices contains a monochromatic triangle.

For the five-vertex assertion, label the vertices \(0,1,2,3,4\). Color red precisely the five edges
\[
\{0,1\},\ \{1,2\},\ \{2,3\},\ \{3,4\},\ \{0,4\},
\]
and color blue precisely the other five edges
\[
\{0,2\},\ \{0,3\},\ \{1,3\},\ \{1,4\},\ \{2,4\}.
\]
The two displayed lists are disjoint and together list every unordered pair of distinct elements of \(\{0,1,2,3,4\}\), so this defines a red-blue coloring of every edge of the complete graph.

There are exactly ten possible triples of vertices. Each has edges of both colors, as the following exhaustive audit shows:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{vertices}&\text{a red edge in the triple}&\text{a blue edge in the triple}\\
\hline
\{0,1,2\}&\{0,1\}&\{0,2\}\\
\{0,1,3\}&\{0,1\}&\{0,3\}\\
\{0,1,4\}&\{0,1\}&\{1,4\}\\
\{0,2,3\}&\{2,3\}&\{0,2\}\\
\{0,2,4\}&\{0,4\}&\{0,2\}\\
\{0,3,4\}&\{3,4\}&\{0,3\}\\
\{1,2,3\}&\{1,2\}&\{1,3\}\\
\{1,2,4\}&\{1,2\}&\{1,4\}\\
\{1,3,4\}&\{3,4\}&\{1,3\}\\
\{2,3,4\}&\{2,3\}&\{2,4\}
\end{array}
\]
Thus no triple of vertices has all three edges red or all three edges blue, so this coloring contains no monochromatic triangle.

By definition, \(R(3,3)\) is the least positive integer for which every red-blue coloring of the complete graph on that many vertices contains a monochromatic triangle. The six-vertex assertion gives \(R(3,3)\le 6\). The five-vertex construction rules out every candidate integer at most five: if the required property held for some positive integer at most five, then in the displayed coloring one could choose that many of the five vertices, and the induced coloring would contain a monochromatic triangle, which would also be a monochromatic triangle in the full five-vertex coloring. This contradicts the exhaustive audit. Hence \(R(3,3)>5\). Since \(R(3,3)\) is an integer, the two inequalities imply \(R(3,3)=6\).

## intuition
At a vertex of the six-vertex graph, three incident edges share a color; their three remote endpoints force a monochromatic triangle in that color or the other. Sharpness is witnessed by coloring a 5-cycle red and its complement blue.
