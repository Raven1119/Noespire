## statement
Let \(G\) be a finite undirected graph. Prove that \(G\) is bipartite if and only if \(G\) contains no cycle of odd length.

## proof
We first prove an internal lemma. A closed walk of odd length contains a cycle of odd length. Indeed, let \(W=(v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_m)\) be a closed walk of positive odd length \(m\), so \(v_0=v_m\). We use strong induction on \(m\). If the vertices \(v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_{m-1}\) are pairwise distinct, then \(W\) itself is a cycle of odd length. Otherwise there are indices \(i,j\) with \(0\leq i<j\leq m\), \(v_i=v_j\), and \(1\leq j-i<m\). The segment from \(v_i\) through \(v_j\) is a closed walk of length \(j-i\). The complementary segment, obtained by going from \(v_j\) through \(v_m=v_0\) and then through \(v_i\), is a closed walk of length \(m-(j-i)\). Both lengths are positive and smaller than \(m\), and their sum is the odd integer \(m\). Hence one of these two closed walks has odd length. By the induction hypothesis, that shorter odd closed walk contains an odd cycle. This proves the lemma.

Suppose first that \(G\) is bipartite, and let \(X\) and \(Y\) be its two vertex classes. Every edge of \(G\) has one endpoint in \(X\) and the other endpoint in \(Y\). Along any cycle the successive vertices therefore alternate between \(X\) and \(Y\). After an odd number of edges one is in the class opposite to the starting class, so returning to the starting vertex requires an even number of edges. Thus every cycle of \(G\) has even length, and \(G\) contains no cycle of odd length.

Conversely, suppose that \(G\) contains no cycle of odd length. By the internal lemma, \(G\) then contains no closed walk of odd length. For each connected component \(H\) of \(G\), choose a root vertex \(r_H\). For every vertex \(x\) of \(H\), choose a path from \(r_H\) to \(x\) and classify \(x\) according as the length of this path is even or odd. This classification does not depend on the chosen path: if two paths \(P\) and \(Q\) from \(r_H\) to the same vertex \(x\) had lengths of opposite parity, then traversing \(P\) and then traversing \(Q\) in reverse would give a closed walk of odd length, contrary to the preceding paragraph.

Let \(X\) be the set of all vertices classified even in their respective connected components, and let \(Y\) be the set of all vertices classified odd. These sets are disjoint and contain every vertex of \(G\). Let \(xy\) be any edge of \(G\). Its endpoints lie in the same connected component \(H\). Choose a path \(P\) from \(r_H\) to \(x\) and a path \(Q\) from \(r_H\) to \(y\). If \(x\) and \(y\) belonged to the same one of \(X\) and \(Y\), then the lengths of \(P\) and \(Q\) would have the same parity. Traversing \(P\), then the edge \(xy\), and then \(Q\) in reverse would be a closed walk whose length is the length of \(P\), plus one, plus the length of \(Q\), and hence would be odd. This is impossible. Consequently every edge has one endpoint in \(X\) and one endpoint in \(Y\), so \(X,Y\) is a bipartition of \(G\). Therefore \(G\) is bipartite.

## intuition
Odd cycles are exactly the obstruction to assigning vertices two colors by parity of path length from a component root. Odd closed walks are used because overlapping paths need not form a simple cycle directly.
