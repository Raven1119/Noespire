## statement
Let \(n\) be a positive integer and let \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) be real numbers. Prove the Vandermonde determinant identity
\[
\det\!\begin{pmatrix}
1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \cdots & x_1^{n-1}\\
1 & x_2 & x_2^2 & \cdots & x_2^{n-1}\\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
1 & x_n & x_n^2 & \cdots & x_n^{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}
=\prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(x_j-x_i).
\]

## proof
Let \(A\) denote the displayed \(n\times n\) matrix. We prove the identity by induction on the positive integer \(n\).

For \(n=1\), the matrix \(A\) is the \(1\times1\) matrix \((1)\), so \(\det A=1\). The product over the empty set of pairs \(1\le i<j\le1\) is, by definition, \(1\). Thus the identity holds when \(n=1\).

Now let \(n\ge2\), and assume that the asserted identity holds for every list of \(n-1\) real numbers. For each integer \(k\) in the descending order \(n,n-1,\ldots,2\), replace column \(C_k\) of \(A\) by
\[
C_k-x_1C_{k-1}.
\]
At the moment this operation is performed, \(C_{k-1}\) is still its original column because the operations are performed in descending order. Replacing one column by itself plus a scalar multiple of a different column does not change the determinant, so the determinant of the resulting matrix is \(\det A\).

In the first row, the entry in every new column \(k\ge2\) is
\[
x_1^{k-1}-x_1x_1^{k-2}=0,
\]
while the first entry remains \(1\). In row \(i\), where \(2\le i\le n\), the entry in every new column \(k\ge2\) is
\[
x_i^{k-1}-x_1x_i^{k-2}=(x_i-x_1)x_i^{k-2}.
\]
Consequently, expansion of the resulting determinant along its first row (whose only nonzero entry is the \(1\) in position \((1,1)\), with positive cofactor sign) gives
\[
\det A
=
\det\!\begin{pmatrix}
x_2-x_1 & (x_2-x_1)x_2 & \cdots & (x_2-x_1)x_2^{n-2}\\
x_3-x_1 & (x_3-x_1)x_3 & \cdots & (x_3-x_1)x_3^{n-2}\\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
x_n-x_1 & (x_n-x_1)x_n & \cdots & (x_n-x_1)x_n^{n-2}
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Factoring \(x_i-x_1\) from the row corresponding to each \(i\in\{2,\ldots,n\}\) yields
\[
\det A
=
\left(\prod_{i=2}^{n}(x_i-x_1)\right)
\det\!\begin{pmatrix}
1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_2^{n-2}\\
1 & x_3 & \cdots & x_3^{n-2}\\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
1 & x_n & \cdots & x_n^{n-2}
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The remaining determinant is the Vandermonde determinant of the \(n-1\) real numbers \(x_2,\ldots,x_n\). By the induction hypothesis it equals
\[
\prod_{2\le i<j\le n}(x_j-x_i).
\]
Therefore
\[
\det A
=
\left(\prod_{i=2}^{n}(x_i-x_1)\right)
\left(\prod_{2\le i<j\le n}(x_j-x_i)\right)
=
\prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(x_j-x_i),
\]
because the first product consists exactly of the factors indexed by pairs with first index \(1\), and the second product consists exactly of the factors indexed by pairs with first index at least \(2\). This completes the induction and the proof.

## intuition
Descending column elimination extracts all factors involving x_1 and leaves the smaller Vandermonde determinant on x_2,...,x_n.
