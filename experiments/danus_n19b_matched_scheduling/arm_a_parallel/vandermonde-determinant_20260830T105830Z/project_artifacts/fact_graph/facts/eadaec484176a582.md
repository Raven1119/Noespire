## statement
Let \(n\) be a positive integer and let \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) be real numbers. Prove the Vandermonde determinant identity
\[
\det\!\begin{pmatrix}
1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \cdots & x_1^{n-1}\\
1 & x_2 & x_2^2 & \cdots & x_2^{n-1}\\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
1 & x_n & x_n^2 & \cdots & x_n^{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}
=\prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(x_j-x_i).
\]

## proof
We prove the identity by induction on the positive integer n.

Base case n=1. The displayed matrix is the 1 by 1 matrix whose only entry is 1, so its determinant is 1. The product over pairs (i,j) satisfying 1<=i<j<=1 has no factors and hence, by the empty-product convention, is also 1.

Induction step. Fix an integer n>=2, and assume that for every choice of n-1 real numbers the claimed identity holds for the corresponding (n-1) by (n-1) Vandermonde matrix. Let A be the displayed n by n matrix, so its entry in row i and column k is x_i^{k-1} for 1<=i<=n and 1<=k<=n.

Starting with k=n and proceeding through k=n-1,...,2 in descending order, replace column k by column k minus x_1 times column k-1. Each such replacement leaves the determinant unchanged, because replacing one column by itself plus a scalar multiple of a different column does not change a determinant. The descending order is important: at the moment column k is replaced, column k-1 is still its original column, since only columns with indices greater than k have previously been replaced.

Let B be the resulting matrix. Its first column is unchanged, so B_{i1}=1 for every i. For 2<=k<=n, the preceding observation about the operation order gives
B_{ik}=x_i^{k-1}-x_1x_i^{k-2}=(x_i-x_1)x_i^{k-2}
for every 1<=i<=n. In particular, when i=1 this entry is 0. Thus the first row of B is (1,0,...,0).

Because all column replacements preserved the determinant, det(A)=det(B). Expanding det(B) along its first row, the only nonzero term comes from the entry B_{11}=1. Its cofactor sign is (-1)^{1+1}=1. Therefore det(A) equals the determinant of the (n-1) by (n-1) matrix B' obtained from B by deleting row 1 and column 1. For row index i in {2,...,n} and column index k in {2,...,n}, the corresponding entry of B' is (x_i-x_1)x_i^{k-2}. Factoring the scalar x_i-x_1 from the row corresponding to each i=2,...,n yields

det(A) = (product from i=2 to n of (x_i-x_1)) det((x_i^{k-2})_{2<=i<=n, 2<=k<=n}).

The determinant on the right is exactly the (n-1) by (n-1) Vandermonde determinant associated with the ordered real numbers x_2,...,x_n: its columns have exponents 0,1,...,n-2. By the induction hypothesis it equals
product over 2<=i<j<=n of (x_j-x_i).
Consequently,

det(A) = (product from j=2 to n of (x_j-x_1))(product over 2<=i<j<=n of (x_j-x_i))
       = product over 1<=i<j<=n of (x_j-x_i),

because the first product consists exactly of all factors indexed by pairs with i=1, while the second consists exactly of all factors indexed by pairs with i>=2. This argument did not divide by any difference x_j-x_i, so it also applies when some of the real numbers x_i coincide. The induction is complete.

## intuition
Descending column subtraction makes every nonfirst entry in row i visibly divisible by x_i-x_1. Removing the first row and column leaves the smaller Vandermonde matrix, so induction accumulates exactly one factor for each ordered pair i<j.
