## statement
Let \(n\) be a positive integer, and let \(\mathcal A\) be a family of subsets of \([n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}\) such that no member of \(\mathcal A\) contains another. Prove that
\[
|\mathcal A|\le \binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor},
\]
and prove that equality is attained by taking all subsets of \([n]\) having size \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\).

## proof
Let \(m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\).  For every permutation
\(\pi=(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_n)\) of \([n]\), form the sequence of initial
segments
\[
 \varnothing,\ \{\pi_1\},\ \{\pi_1,\pi_2\},\ \ldots,\
 \{\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_n\}=[n].
\]
There are \(n!\) permutations of \([n]\).

Define an incidence to be a pair \((A,\pi)\) such that
\(A\in\mathcal A\), \(\pi\) is a permutation of \([n]\), and \(A\) is
one of the displayed initial segments for \(\pi\).  Fix
\(A\in\mathcal A\) and put \(k=|A|\).  The set \(A\) is an initial
segment for \(\pi\) precisely when the elements of \(A\) occupy the
first \(k\) positions of \(\pi\), in some order, and the elements of
\([n]\setminus A\) occupy the remaining \(n-k\) positions, in some
order.  Consequently, \(A\) is incident with exactly
\(k!(n-k)!\) permutations.

For a fixed permutation \(\pi\), its initial segments are linearly
ordered by containment.  Therefore two distinct members of
\(\mathcal A\) cannot both be initial segments for the same \(\pi\),
because that would make one contain the other.  Thus every permutation
is incident with at most one member of \(\mathcal A\).  Counting the
incidences first by \(A\) and then by \(\pi\) gives
\[
 \sum_{A\in\mathcal A}|A|!(n-|A|)!\le n!.
\]
Since
\[
 \binom{n}{|A|}=\frac{n!}{|A|!(n-|A|)!},
\]
division by \(n!\) yields
\[
 \sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{|A|}}\le 1. \tag{1}
\]

It remains to compare the binomial coefficients.  If \(0\le j<m\),
then \(2j+1\le 2m-1\le n\), so \(n-j\ge j+1\), and hence
\[
 \frac{\binom{n}{j+1}}{\binom{n}{j}}
 =\frac{n-j}{j+1}\ge 1.
\]
It follows successively that
\(\binom{n}{k}\le\binom{n}{m}\) for every integer \(k\) with
\(0\le k\le m\).  If \(m<k\le n\), then \(0\le n-k\le m\), and the
factorial formula gives
\[
 \binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}\le\binom{n}{m}.
\]
Thus, for every \(A\in\mathcal A\),
\[
 \frac{1}{\binom{n}{|A|}}\ge\frac{1}{\binom{n}{m}}.
\]
Combining this inequality with (1) gives
\[
 \frac{|\mathcal A|}{\binom{n}{m}}
 \le \sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{|A|}}
 \le 1,
\]
and therefore
\[
 |\mathcal A|\le\binom{n}{m}
 =\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.
\]

Finally, let
\[
 \mathcal B=\{B\subseteq[n]:|B|=m\}.
\]
If two distinct members \(B_1,B_2\in\mathcal B\) satisfied
\(B_1\subseteq B_2\), then the equality
\(|B_1|=|B_2|=m\) would force \(B_1=B_2\), a contradiction.
Hence no member of \(\mathcal B\) contains another.  By the definition
of the binomial coefficient,
\[
 |\mathcal B|=\binom{n}{m}
 =\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.
\]
Thus this family attains equality.

## intuition
A uniformly chosen maximal chain meets an antichain at most once. Double-counting the incidences between antichain members and permutation-generated maximal chains gives the LYM inequality; the largest binomial layer is the middle one.
