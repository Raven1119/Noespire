## statement
Let \(n\) be a positive integer, and let \(\mathcal A\) be a family of subsets of \([n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}\) such that no member of \(\mathcal A\) contains another. Prove that
\[
|\mathcal A|\le \binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor},
\]
and prove that equality is attained by taking all subsets of \([n]\) having size \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\).

## proof
For each \(A\in\mathcal A\), put \(k_A=|A|\), and let \(P_A\) be the set of all permutations \(\pi=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) of \([n]\) for which
\[
A=\{x_1,\ldots,x_{k_A}\}.
\]
To form such a permutation, one may arrange the elements of \(A\) in its first \(k_A\) positions in \(k_A!\) ways and arrange the elements of \([n]\setminus A\) in its remaining positions in \((n-k_A)!\) ways. Hence
\[
|P_A|=k_A!(n-k_A)!.
\]

The sets \(P_A\), as \(A\) ranges over \(\mathcal A\), are pairwise disjoint. Indeed, suppose that a permutation belongs to both \(P_A\) and \(P_B\), and assume without loss of generality that \(k_A\le k_B\). Then
\[
A=\{x_1,\ldots,x_{k_A}\}\subseteq \{x_1,\ldots,x_{k_B}\}=B.
\]
The hypothesis on \(\mathcal A\) therefore forces \(A=B\). Since there are exactly \(n!\) permutations of \([n]\), it follows that
\[
\sum_{A\in\mathcal A} k_A!(n-k_A)!\le n!.
\]
Dividing by \(n!\) and using \(\binom{n}{k_A}=n!/(k_A!(n-k_A)!)\), we obtain
\[
\sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k_A}}\le 1. \tag{1}
\]

We next verify directly that the largest binomial coefficient in row \(n\) is a middle one. For every integer \(j\) with \(0\le j<n\),
\[
\frac{\binom{n}{j+1}}{\binom{n}{j}}=\frac{n-j}{j+1}.
\]
This ratio is at least \(1\) whenever \(j\le (n-1)/2\). Together with the symmetry \(\binom{n}{j}=\binom{n}{n-j}\), this proves that
\[
\binom{n}{j}\le M:=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
\]
for every integer \(j\) with \(0\le j\le n\). Consequently every summand in (1) is at least \(1/M\), and hence
\[
\frac{|\mathcal A|}{M}\le \sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k_A}}\le 1.
\]
Thus \(|\mathcal A|\le \binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\).

Finally, let \(\mathcal B\) be the family of all subsets of \([n]\) having cardinality \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\). It has exactly \(\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\) members. If two distinct members of \(\mathcal B\) contained one another, their equal finite cardinalities would force them to be equal, a contradiction. Thus \(\mathcal B\) satisfies the hypothesis and attains equality.

## intuition
A permutation determines a maximal chain of initial-segment subsets. An antichain meets each such chain at most once. Double-counting incidences between family members and these chains gives the LYM inequality, from which the central-layer bound follows because the middle binomial coefficient is maximal.
