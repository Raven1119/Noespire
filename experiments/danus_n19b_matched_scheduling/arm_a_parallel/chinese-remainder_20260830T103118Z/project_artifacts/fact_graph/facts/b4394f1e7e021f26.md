## statement
Let \(m_1,\ldots,m_k\) be pairwise coprime integers greater than \(1\), and let \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\) be arbitrary integers. Prove that there is an integer \(x\) satisfying
\[
x\equiv a_i\pmod{m_i}\qquad(1\le i\le k),
\]
and that any two such integers are congruent modulo \(M=m_1m_2\cdots m_k\).

## proof
We use Bézout's identity in the following explicit form: whenever integers r and s satisfy gcd(r,s)=1, there exist integers c and e such that cr+es=1.

Put M=m_1m_2\cdots m_k. For each i with 1<=i<=k, put M_i=M/m_i, so M_i is the product of all m_j with j different from i (with the empty product interpreted as 1 when k=1).

We first construct a solution. Fix i. For each j different from i, pairwise coprimality and Bézout's identity give integers p_{ij},q_{ij} such that
p_{ij}m_j+q_{ij}m_i=1.
Multiply these identities over all j different from i. In the expansion of the product, the term obtained by choosing p_{ij}m_j from every factor is (product_{j different from i} p_{ij})M_i. Every other term contains at least one factor q_{ij}m_i and is therefore divisible by m_i. Consequently there exist integers u_i,v_i such that
u_iM_i+v_im_i=1.
(The same conclusion for k=1 is given by u_1=1 and v_1=0.) In particular, u_iM_i is congruent to 1 modulo m_i.

Define the integer
x_0=sum_{i=1}^k a_i u_i M_i.
Fix r with 1<=r<=k. If i is different from r, then m_r is one of the factors of M_i, hence a_i u_i M_i is divisible by m_r. The remaining term satisfies a_r u_rM_r congruent to a_r modulo m_r because u_rM_r is congruent to 1 modulo m_r. Thus x_0 is congruent to a_r modulo m_r. Since r was arbitrary, x_0 satisfies all the required congruences. This proves existence.

It remains to prove uniqueness modulo M. Let x and y be any two integers satisfying all the congruences, and put d=x-y. For every i, both x and y are congruent to a_i modulo m_i, so m_i divides d.

For 1<=t<=k define P_t=m_1m_2\cdots m_t. We prove by induction on t that P_t divides d. The assertion for t=1 is exactly m_1 divides d. Suppose 1<=t<k and P_t divides d. For each j with 1<=j<=t, pairwise coprimality and Bézout's identity give integers A_j,B_j such that
A_jm_j+B_jm_{t+1}=1.
Multiplying these t identities and expanding, the term obtained by choosing A_jm_j from every factor is (product_{j=1}^t A_j)P_t, while every other term is divisible by m_{t+1}. Hence there are integers C_t,D_t satisfying
C_tP_t+D_tm_{t+1}=1.
Multiplication by d gives
d=C_tP_td+D_tm_{t+1}d.
The first term on the right is divisible by P_tm_{t+1}, because m_{t+1} divides d. The second term is divisible by P_tm_{t+1}, because P_t divides d by the induction hypothesis. Therefore P_tm_{t+1}=P_{t+1} divides d. Induction gives P_k=M divides x-y. Equivalently, x is congruent to y modulo M. Thus any two simultaneous solutions are congruent modulo M, as required.

## intuition
The complementary product M_i vanishes modulo every modulus except m_i; multiplying it by its inverse modulo m_i creates a selector for the i-th residue. Pairwise coprimality also ensures that divisibility by every modulus combines into divisibility by their product.
