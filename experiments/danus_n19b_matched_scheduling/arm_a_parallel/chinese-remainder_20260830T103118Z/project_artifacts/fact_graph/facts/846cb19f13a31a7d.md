## statement
Let \(m_1,\ldots,m_k\) be pairwise coprime integers greater than \(1\), and let \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\) be arbitrary integers. Prove that there is an integer \(x\) satisfying
\[
x\equiv a_i\pmod{m_i}\qquad(1\le i\le k),
\]
and that any two such integers are congruent modulo \(M=m_1m_2\cdots m_k\).

## proof
Let k be the number of moduli, so k is a positive integer. We first prove the form of Bézout's identity needed below, directly from the hypotheses. Let A and B be coprime positive integers. The set
S={rA+sB : r,s are integers and rA+sB>0}
is nonempty because A belongs to S. By the well-ordering principle, S has a least member δ; write δ=r_0A+s_0B. By integer division, A=qδ+t for some integers q,t with 0≤t<δ. Then
 t=A-qδ=(1-qr_0)A+(-qs_0)B.
If t>0, then t belongs to S and is smaller than δ, contrary to the definition of δ. Hence t=0, so δ divides A. The same argument, dividing B by δ, shows that δ divides B. Since A and B are coprime and δ is positive, δ=1. Thus r_0A+s_0B=1.

We also record two consequences, proving both explicitly. First, if A and B are each coprime to a positive integer C, then AB is coprime to C. Indeed, the preceding paragraph supplies integers r,s,u,v such that rA+sC=1 and uB+vC=1. Multiplication gives
 1=(rA+sC)(uB+vC)=ruAB+C(rvA+suB+svC).
Thus any positive common divisor of AB and C divides 1, so it equals 1. Induction therefore shows that a finite product of positive integers, each coprime to C, is coprime to C; the empty product 1 is also coprime to C.

Second, suppose A and B are coprime positive integers and both divide an integer d. Choose integers r,s with rA+sB=1, and write d=Aq=Bp for integers p,q. Multiplying the identity by d gives
 d=rAd+sBd=rA(Bp)+sB(Aq)=AB(rp+sq).
Consequently AB divides d.

Now set M=m_1m_2\cdots m_k. For each i with 1≤i≤k, define
 N_i=M/m_i=\prod_{1≤j≤k,\ j\ne i}m_j,
where the product is 1 if k=1. Every factor m_j of N_i is coprime to m_i by pairwise coprimality. By the first consequence above, N_i is coprime to m_i. The proved Bézout identity therefore supplies integers u_i,v_i such that
 u_iN_i+v_im_i=1.
Define the integer
 x=\sum_{i=1}^k a_i u_iN_i.
Fix an index ℓ with 1≤ℓ≤k. The Bézout identity for i=ℓ gives u_ℓN_ℓ≡1 (mod m_ℓ). If i≠ℓ, then m_ℓ is a factor of N_i, so u_iN_i≡0 (mod m_ℓ). Reducing the defining sum for x modulo m_ℓ therefore gives
 x≡a_ℓu_ℓN_ℓ+\sum_{i\ne ℓ}a_i u_iN_i≡a_ℓ (mod m_ℓ).
Since ℓ was arbitrary, x satisfies all k required congruences.

It remains to prove uniqueness modulo M. Let x and y be two integers satisfying all the congruences, and put d=x-y. Then m_i divides d for every i. We prove by induction on r, for 1≤r≤k, that P_r=m_1m_2\cdots m_r divides d. For r=1 this is the already known divisibility m_1|d. Suppose P_r|d for some r<k. Each of m_1,...,m_r is coprime to m_{r+1}, so the first consequence above shows that P_r is coprime to m_{r+1}. Also m_{r+1}|d. Applying the second consequence above with A=P_r and B=m_{r+1} yields P_rm_{r+1}=P_{r+1}|d. This completes the induction. Taking r=k gives M|x-y, which is exactly x≡y (mod M).

## intuition
Construct, for each modulus, an integer u_iN_i that is 1 modulo m_i and 0 modulo every other modulus. Their residue-weighted sum is the desired solution. Pairwise coprimality also forces the product of all moduli to divide the difference of any two solutions.
