## statement
Let \(m_1,\ldots,m_k\) be pairwise coprime integers greater than \(1\), and let \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\) be arbitrary integers. Prove that there is an integer \(x\) satisfying
\[
x\equiv a_i\pmod{m_i}\qquad(1\le i\le k),
\]
and that any two such integers are congruent modulo \(M=m_1m_2\cdots m_k\).

## proof
We take \(k\ge 1\).

First we establish the version of Bezout's identity needed below. If \(p,q\) are positive integers, let
\[
S=\{up+vq:u,v\in\mathbb Z,\ up+vq>0\}.
\]
The set \(S\) is nonempty because \(p\in S\), so it has a least element \(d=u_0p+v_0q\). By integer division, \(p=cd+r\) for some integers \(c,r\) with \(0\le r<d\). Then
\[
r=p-cd=(1-cu_0)p-cv_0q.
\]
If \(r>0\), this displays \(r\) as an element of \(S\) smaller than \(d\), a contradiction. Hence \(r=0\), so \(d\mid p\). The same argument with \(q\) in place of \(p\) gives \(d\mid q\). Conversely, every common divisor of \(p\) and \(q\) divides the integer linear combination \(d=u_0p+v_0q\). Thus \(d=\gcd(p,q)\). In particular, if \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime, there are integers \(u,v\) such that \(up+vq=1\).

Define
\[
M_i=\prod_{\substack{1\le j\le k\\j\ne i}}m_j
\]
for \(1\le i\le k\), with an empty product equal to \(1\). Fix \(i\). For every \(j\ne i\), pairwise coprimality and the preceding Bezout argument give integers \(u_{ij},v_{ij}\) such that
\[
u_{ij}m_i+v_{ij}m_j=1.
\]
Multiply these identities over all \(j\ne i\). In the expansion of the product, the term obtained by choosing \(v_{ij}m_j\) from every factor is
\[
\left(\prod_{j\ne i}v_{ij}\right)M_i.
\]
Every other term contains at least one factor \(u_{ij}m_i\), and hence the sum of all the other terms is divisible by \(m_i\). Therefore there exist integers \(A_i,N_i\) such that
\[
A_im_i+N_iM_i=1. \tag{1}
\]
For \(k=1\), this conclusion holds with \(M_1=N_1=1\) and \(A_1=0\).

Set
\[
x_0=\sum_{i=1}^k a_iN_iM_i.
\]
Fix \(r\) with \(1\le r\le k\). If \(i\ne r\), then \(M_i\) contains the factor \(m_r\), so \(m_r\mid a_iN_iM_i\). Equation (1) for \(i=r\) gives \(N_rM_r-1=-A_rm_r\), so \(m_r\mid a_r(N_rM_r-1)\). Consequently
\[
x_0-a_r=a_r(N_rM_r-1)+\sum_{\substack{1\le i\le k\\i\ne r}}a_iN_iM_i
\]
is divisible by \(m_r\). Hence \(x_0\equiv a_r\pmod{m_r}\). Since \(r\) was arbitrary, \(x_0\) satisfies all the required congruences.

It remains to prove uniqueness modulo the full product. Let \(x\) and \(y\) both satisfy all the congruences, and put \(n=x-y\). Then \(m_i\mid n\) for every \(i\).

We prove by induction on \(r\) that
\[
P_r:=\prod_{i=1}^r m_i
\]
divides \(n\). The assertion for \(r=1\) is \(m_1\mid n\), already known. Suppose \(1\le r<k\) and \(P_r\mid n\). For each \(i\) with \(1\le i\le r\), pairwise coprimality and Bezout's identity give integers \(\alpha_i,\beta_i\) such that
\[
\alpha_im_i+\beta_im_{r+1}=1.
\]
Multiply these \(r\) identities. The term obtained by choosing \(\alpha_im_i\) from every factor is \((\prod_{i=1}^r\alpha_i)P_r\), while every other term is divisible by \(m_{r+1}\). Thus there are integers \(C,D\) such that
\[
CP_r+Dm_{r+1}=1. \tag{2}
\]
Write \(n=P_rq\) with \(q\in\mathbb Z\). Multiplying (2) by \(q\) gives
\[
q=Cn+Dm_{r+1}q.
\]
Both terms on the right are divisible by \(m_{r+1}\): the first because \(m_{r+1}\mid n\), and the second by its displayed factor. Therefore \(m_{r+1}\mid q\), so \(P_{r+1}=P_rm_{r+1}\mid n\). This completes the induction. Taking \(r=k\) yields
\[
M=m_1m_2\cdots m_k\mid x-y.
\]
Therefore \(x\equiv y\pmod M\), as required.

## intuition
Construct idempotent-like coefficients N_iM_i that are 1 modulo m_i and 0 modulo every other modulus. Uniqueness follows because pairwise-coprime divisibility accumulates multiplicatively.
