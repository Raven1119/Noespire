## statement
For which real polynomials \(p\) is there a real polynomial \(q\) such that

\[
p(p(x))-x=(p(x)-x)^2q(x)
\]

for all real \(x\)?

## proof
The answer is
\[
\boxed{\,p(x)=x+c\quad\text{or}\quad p(x)=-x+c,\qquad c\in\mathbb R\,}.
\]

Necessity. Define \(r(x)=p(x)-x\), so \(p(x)=x+r(x)\). Then
\[
p(p(x))-x=p(x)+r(p(x))-x=r(x)+r(x+r(x)). \tag{1}
\]
For any real polynomial \(r\), there is a real polynomial \(s\) satisfying
\[
r(x+r(x))=r(x)+r'(x)r(x)+r(x)^2s(x). \tag{2}
\]
To justify (2), write \(r(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n a_kx^k\) and expand each
\[
(x+r(x))^k=x^k+kx^{k-1}r(x)+r(x)^2
 \sum_{j=2}^k\binom{k}{j}x^{k-j}r(x)^{j-2}.
\]
After multiplication by \(a_k\) and summation, the first two terms are \(r(x)\) and \(r'(x)r(x)\), and the remaining sum is \(r(x)^2s(x)\) for a polynomial \(s\).

Combining (1) and (2) gives
\[
p(p(x))-x=r(x)(2+r'(x))+r(x)^2s(x). \tag{3}
\]
If a polynomial \(q\) as in the question exists, then (3) yields
\[
r(x)^2(q(x)-s(x))=r(x)(2+r'(x)). \tag{4}
\]
If \(r=0\), then \(p(x)=x\), which is included in the first displayed family. Suppose \(r\ne0\). Since \(\mathbb R[x]\) is an integral domain, (4) may be cancelled by \(r\), giving
\[
r(x)(q(x)-s(x))=2+r'(x). \tag{5}
\]
Thus \(r\) divides \(2+r'\).

If \(r\) is constant, write \(r(x)=c\), so \(p(x)=x+c\). If \(r\) is nonconstant, then either \(2+r'=0\), or
\[
\deg(2+r')\leq \deg r-1<\deg r.
\]
The latter alternative is incompatible with the divisibility in (5), because a nonzero multiple of a nonconstant polynomial \(r\) has degree at least \(\deg r\). Hence \(2+r'=0\). It follows that \(r(x)=-2x+c\) for some \(c\in\mathbb R\), and therefore \(p(x)=-x+c\).

Sufficiency. If \(p(x)=x+c\), then
\[
p(p(x))-x=2c,\qquad (p(x)-x)^2=c^2.
\]
For \(c\ne0\), the constant polynomial \(q(x)=2/c\) gives the required identity; for \(c=0\), take \(q=0\). If \(p(x)=-x+c\), then \(p(p(x))=x\), so \(q=0\) gives the required identity. Thus every polynomial in the two displayed families works, and the necessity argument proves there are no others.

## intuition
Writing p as the identity map plus r turns composition into a first-order expansion modulo r squared. The requested square divisibility then forces r to divide 2+r', which is impossible by degree unless r is constant or has derivative -2.
