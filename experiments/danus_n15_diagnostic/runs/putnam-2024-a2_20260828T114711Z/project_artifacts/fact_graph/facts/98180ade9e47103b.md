## statement
For which real polynomials \(p\) is there a real polynomial \(q\) such that

\[
p(p(x))-x=(p(x)-x)^2q(x)
\]

for all real \(x\)?

## proof
The answer is precisely
\[
 p(x)=x+c\quad\text{or}\quad p(x)=-x+c,
\]
where in either family \(c\) is an arbitrary real number.

Necessity. Let \(p,q\in\mathbb R[x]\) satisfy the displayed identity for every \(x\in\mathbb R\). If \(p(x)=x\) for every real \(x\), then \(p\) belongs to the first claimed family with \(c=0\). Hence suppose that the polynomial
\[
 r(x):=p(x)-x
\]
is nonzero.

We first establish an exact polynomial expansion. For every nonnegative integer \(k\), the polynomial
\[
 (x+y)^k-x^k-kx^{k-1}y
\]
(with the evident zero interpretation of the linear term when \(k=0\)) is divisible by \(y^2\): for \(k=0,1\) it is zero, and for \(k\ge 2\) this follows by writing the binomial expansion and retaining the terms whose powers of \(y\) are at least two. Applying this to every monomial of \(p\), there is a polynomial \(S\in\mathbb R[x,y]\) such that
\[
 p(x+y)=p(x)+y p'(x)+y^2S(x,y).
\]
Substitute \(y=r(x)\). Since \(p(x)=x+r(x)\), this gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 p(p(x))-x
 &=p(x+r(x))-x\\
 &=p(x)-x+r(x)p'(x)+r(x)^2S(x,r(x))\\
 &=r(x)(1+p'(x))+r(x)^2S(x,r(x)).
\end{aligned}
\]
Comparing this equality with the assumed equality \(p(p(x))-x=r(x)^2q(x)\), we obtain
\[
 r(x)(1+p'(x))=r(x)^2\bigl(q(x)-S(x,r(x))\bigr).
\]
The ring \(\mathbb R[x]\) is an integral domain and \(r\) is nonzero, so cancellation of \(r\) yields
\[
 1+p'(x)=r(x)\bigl(q(x)-S(x,r(x))\bigr).
\]
Thus \(p(x)-x\) divides \(1+p'(x)\) in \(\mathbb R[x]\).

Suppose that \(n:=\deg p\ge 2\). Then \(\deg(p-x)=n\), while \(1+p'\) is nonzero and has degree \(n-1\). A nonzero polynomial of degree \(n\) cannot divide a nonzero polynomial of degree \(n-1\). This contradiction proves that \(\deg p\le 1\).

Consequently there are real numbers \(a,b\) such that \(p(x)=ax+b\). The divisibility just proved becomes
\[
 (a-1)x+b\ \mid\ a+1.
\]
If \(a=1\), then \(p(x)=x+b\), which is the first claimed family. If \(a\ne 1\), the divisor \((a-1)x+b\) has degree one. Such a polynomial cannot divide a nonzero constant, so \(a+1=0\). Hence \(a=-1\), and \(p(x)=-x+b\), which is the second claimed family. This also excludes every constant polynomial.

Sufficiency. Let \(c\in\mathbb R\). If \(p(x)=x+c\) and \(c\ne0\), choose the constant polynomial \(q(x)=2/c\). Then \(p(p(x))-x=2c\) and \((p(x)-x)^2q(x)=c^2(2/c)=2c\). If \(p(x)=x\), choose \(q(x)=0\); both sides are zero. Finally, if \(p(x)=-x+c\), choose \(q(x)=0\). In that case \(p(p(x))=x\), so again both sides are zero. Therefore every polynomial in the two claimed families works, and the necessity argument proves that no others work.

## intuition
Modulo the square of p(x)-x, composing p with itself only retains the first-order Taylor term. The condition is therefore equivalent (away from the identity case) to p-x dividing p'+1, and degrees force p to be affine.
