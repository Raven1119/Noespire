## statement
Determine all positive integers \(n\) for which there exist positive integers \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) satisfying

\[
2a^n+3b^n=4c^n.
\]

## proof
We prove that the complete set of such positive integers is \(\{1\}\).

First, \(n=1\) works: choose \(a=1\), \(b=2\), and \(c=2\). Then
\[
2a+3b=2\cdot 1+3\cdot 2=8=4\cdot 2=4c.
\]

It remains to prove that no \(n\ge 2\) works.

Case 1: \(n=2\). Suppose, for contradiction, that positive integers \(a,b,c\) satisfy
\[
2a^2+3b^2=4c^2.
\]
Among all such triples choose one for which the positive integer \(a+b+c\) is minimal. Reducing the equation modulo \(3\) gives
\[
2a^2\equiv c^2\pmod 3,
\]
because \(4\equiv 1\pmod 3\). Every integer square is congruent to either \(0\) or \(1\) modulo \(3\). If \(3\nmid a\), then \(a^2\equiv 1\pmod 3\), so the left side is congruent to \(2\pmod 3\), whereas \(c^2\) is congruent to \(0\) or \(1\pmod 3\), a contradiction. Hence \(3\mid a\). The displayed congruence then gives \(c^2\equiv 0\pmod 3\), hence \(3\mid c\). Therefore \(9\) divides both \(2a^2\) and \(4c^2\). From the original equation, \(9\mid 3b^2\), so \(3\mid b^2\), and consequently \(3\mid b\). Write \(a=3A\), \(b=3B\), and \(c=3C\), where \(A,B,C\) are positive integers. Substitution gives
\[
9(2A^2+3B^2)=36C^2,
\]
and division by \(9\) gives
\[
2A^2+3B^2=4C^2.
\]
Thus \((A,B,C)\) is another positive-integer solution, but
\[
A+B+C=(a+b+c)/3<a+b+c,
\]
contradicting the choice of \((a,b,c)\). Hence \(n=2\) does not work.

Case 2: \(n\ge 3\). Suppose, for contradiction, that positive integers \(a,b,c\) satisfy
\[
2a^n+3b^n=4c^n.
\]
Among all such triples for this fixed \(n\), choose one for which \(a+b+c\) is minimal. Reducing the equation modulo \(2\) gives \(b^n\equiv 0\pmod 2\), so \(b\) is even. Write \(b=2B\), where \(B\) is a positive integer. Substituting and dividing by \(2\) gives
\[
a^n+3\cdot 2^{n-1}B^n=2c^n.
\]
Since \(n\ge3\), the second term on the left is even; hence \(a^n\) is even, so \(a\) is even. Write \(a=2A\), where \(A\) is a positive integer. Substituting \(a=2A\) and \(b=2B\) into the original equation gives
\[
2^{n}(2A^n+3B^n)=4c^n,
\]
so
\[
2^{n-2}(2A^n+3B^n)=c^n.
\]
Because \(n\ge3\), the left side is even. Thus \(c^n\) is even, so \(c\) is even. Write \(c=2C\), where \(C\) is a positive integer. Substitution into the preceding equality gives
\[
2^{n-2}(2A^n+3B^n)=2^nC^n.
\]
Cancelling \(2^{n-2}\) yields
\[
2A^n+3B^n=4C^n.
\]
Thus \((A,B,C)\) is another positive-integer solution for the same exponent \(n\), but
\[
A+B+C=(a+b+c)/2<a+b+c,
\]
again contradicting minimality. Hence no \(n\ge3\) works.

Therefore the only positive integer \(n\) satisfying the requirement is \(\boxed{1}\).

## intuition
The exceptional low exponent n=1 has a direct solution. At n=2, reduction modulo 3 forces all three variables to be divisible by 3. At every n≥3, parity forces all three variables to be divisible by 2. Homogeneity then produces a smaller solution of the same equation, contradicting well-ordering.
