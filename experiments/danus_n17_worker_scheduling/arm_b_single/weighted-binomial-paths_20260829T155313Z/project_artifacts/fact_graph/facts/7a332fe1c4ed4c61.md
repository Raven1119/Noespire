## statement
Prove that for every nonnegative integer \(k\),
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{k}2^{k-j}\binom{k+j}{j}=4^k.
\]

## proof
Fix an arbitrary nonnegative integer \(k\). Consider all words of length \(2k+1\) over the two-symbol alphabet \(\{H,T\}\). There are \(2^{2k+1}\) such words.

Because \(2k+1\) is odd, every such word has exactly one of the following two properties: it contains at least \(k+1\) occurrences of \(H\), or it contains at least \(k+1\) occurrences of \(T\). Call these the \(H\)-majority and \(T\)-majority words, respectively. The map that replaces every \(H\) by \(T\) and every \(T\) by \(H\) is a bijection from the set of \(H\)-majority words to the set of \(T\)-majority words. These two sets partition all words of length \(2k+1\). Therefore the number of \(H\)-majority words is
\[
\frac{2^{2k+1}}{2}=2^{2k}=4^k.
\]

We now count the same \(H\)-majority words a second way. In an \(H\)-majority word, consider the unique occurrence which is the \((k+1)\)-st occurrence of \(H\) when the word is read from left to right. Let \(j\) be the number of occurrences of \(T\) preceding this occurrence. The portion preceding it contains exactly \(k\) occurrences of \(H\) and \(j\) occurrences of \(T\), hence has length \(k+j\). Also \(0\le j\le k\), because an \(H\)-majority word of length \(2k+1\) contains at most \(k\) occurrences of \(T\).

For a fixed \(j\) with \(0\le j\le k\), the portion preceding the \((k+1)\)-st \(H\) can be chosen in
\[
\binom{k+j}{j}
\]
ways: choose which \(j\) of its \(k+j\) positions contain \(T\), with all other positions containing \(H\). The next position is forced to contain \(H\). After that position, the number of remaining positions is
\[
(2k+1)-(k+j+1)=k-j,
\]
and each remaining position may independently contain either \(H\) or \(T\). Thus there are
\[
2^{k-j}\binom{k+j}{j}
\]
\(H\)-majority words for which exactly \(j\) occurrences of \(T\) precede the \((k+1)\)-st \(H\). Conversely, every word constructed in this way already contains \(k+1\) occurrences of \(H\), so it is \(H\)-majority. The value of \(j\) is unique for each \(H\)-majority word, so the classes for \(j=0,1,\ldots,k\) are pairwise disjoint and exhaustive.

Consequently the number of \(H\)-majority words is
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{k}2^{k-j}\binom{k+j}{j}.
\]
Equating the two counts of the same set gives
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{k}2^{k-j}\binom{k+j}{j}=4^k.
\]
Since \(k\) was an arbitrary nonnegative integer, the identity holds for every nonnegative integer \(k\).

## intuition
Count binary words of odd length with H as the strict-majority symbol, and classify them by where the (k+1)-st H occurs.
