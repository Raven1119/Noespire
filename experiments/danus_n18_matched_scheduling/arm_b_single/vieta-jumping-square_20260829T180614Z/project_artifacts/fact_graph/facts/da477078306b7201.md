## statement
Let (a) and (b) be positive integers. Suppose that

\[
\frac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}
\]

is an integer. Prove that this integer is a perfect square.

## proof
Let a and b be arbitrary positive integers satisfying the hypothesis, and define
\[
k=\frac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}.
\]
The numerator and denominator are positive, so the assumed integer k is positive. Multiplying by ab+1 gives
\[
a^2+b^2=k(ab+1).
\]
If k=1, then k=1^2 is a perfect square. It remains to treat k\ge 2.

Let
\[
S=\{(r,s): r,s\text{ are positive integers and }r^2+s^2=k(rs+1)\}.
\]
The pair (a,b) belongs to S, so S is nonempty. The set of positive integers
\[
\{\max\{r,s\}:(r,s)\in S\}
\]
therefore has a least element by the well-ordering principle. Choose (x,y)\in S for which \max\{x,y\} is this least element. The defining equation of S is symmetric in the two coordinates, so, after exchanging x and y if necessary, we may and do have x\ge y.

Define the integer
\[
z=ky-x.
\]
Because (x,y)\in S,
\[
x^2+y^2=k(xy+1).
\]
Consequently
\[
xz=x(ky-x)=kxy-x^2=y^2-k. \tag{1}
\]
In particular, x+z=ky and xz=y^2-k. Thus the polynomial
\[
P(t)=t^2-kyt+y^2-k
\]
factors as
\[
P(t)=(t-x)(t-z). \tag{2}
\]

We first prove z\ge 0. Suppose instead that z<0. Since z is an integer, -z\ge 1. Equation (1) then gives
\[
k-y^2=x(-z)\ge x.
\]
On the other hand, z=ky-x gives
\[
x=ky-z\ge k+1,
\]
because y\ge 1 and -z\ge 1. Hence k-y^2\ge k+1. This is impossible, since y\ge 1 also gives k-y^2\le k-1. Therefore z\ge 0.

We next prove z<y. Evaluating P at y and using k\ge 2 gives
\[
P(y)=(2-k)y^2-k<0. \tag{3}
\]
If x=y, then P(y)=P(x)=0 by (2), contradicting (3). Therefore x>y, so y-x<0. Equations (2) and (3) give
\[
(y-x)(y-z)=P(y)<0.
\]
Because y-x<0, this inequality forces y-z>0. Hence z<y.

We now have 0\le z<y. If z=0, equation (1) yields 0=y^2-k, and therefore
\[
k=y^2,
\]
which is a perfect square.

Suppose, for contradiction, that z>0. Since P(z)=0 by (2),
\[
z^2-kyz+y^2-k=0,
\]
or equivalently
\[
y^2+z^2=k(yz+1).
\]
Thus (y,z)\in S. Since 0<z<y<x, we have
\[
\max\{y,z\}=y<x=\max\{x,y\},
\]
contradicting the minimal choice of (x,y). Therefore z>0 is impossible, so z=0, and the preceding paragraph shows k=y^2.

Thus in every case the original integer k=(a^2+b^2)/(ab+1) is a perfect square.

## intuition
Fix the quotient and choose the smallest positive solution. Replacing the larger coordinate x by the other quadratic root z=ky-x preserves the equation. Sign estimates show 0<=z<y. Positivity would contradict minimality, so z=0, and the root-product identity gives k=y^2.
