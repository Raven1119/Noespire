## statement
Let (x,y,z) be positive integers with

\[
x^2+y^2=z^2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\gcd(x,y,z)=1.
\]

Prove that, after possibly interchanging (x) and (y), there are coprime positive integers (m>n) of opposite parity such that

\[
x=m^2-n^2,\qquad y=2mn,\qquad z=m^2+n^2.
\]

Also prove the converse: every pair (m>n) with these properties gives a primitive Pythagorean triple by the displayed formulas.

## proof
Assume first that x,y,z are positive integers satisfying x^2+y^2=z^2 and gcd(x,y,z)=1.

First, x,y,z are pairwise coprime. Indeed, if a prime p divided any two of x,y,z, then the equation would imply that p divides the square of the third integer. Since p is prime, p would divide that third integer as well. Then p would divide x,y,z simultaneously, contrary to gcd(x,y,z)=1.

The integers x and y cannot both be even, by pairwise coprimality. They also cannot both be odd: the square of every odd integer is congruent to 1 modulo 4, so x^2+y^2 would be congruent to 2 modulo 4, whereas an integer square is congruent only to 0 or 1 modulo 4. Therefore exactly one of x,y is even. Interchange x and y if necessary so that y is even. Then x is odd, and z is odd because z^2=x^2+y^2 is congruent to 1 modulo 4.

Let k=y/2 and define
A=(z+x)/2 and B=(z-x)/2.
Because z and x are odd, A and B are integers. Moreover, z^2=x^2+y^2>x^2 and z,x are positive, so z>x. Hence A and B are positive, and A>B. Their product is
AB=((z+x)(z-x))/4=(z^2-x^2)/4=y^2/4=k^2.
They are coprime: every positive integer dividing both A and B divides A+B=z and A-B=x, and gcd(x,z)=1, so their only positive common divisor is 1.

We now show directly that A and B are squares. In the prime factorizations of A and B, no prime occurs in both because gcd(A,B)=1. Since AB=k^2 is a square, every prime has an even exponent in the prime factorization of AB. Consequently, every exponent in the prime factorization of A is even and every exponent in the prime factorization of B is even. Thus there are positive integers m and n such that A=m^2 and B=n^2. Since A>B, positivity gives m>n.

Adding and subtracting the equations A=m^2 and B=n^2 gives
z=A+B=m^2+n^2
and
x=A-B=m^2-n^2.
Also k^2=AB=m^2n^2=(mn)^2. Since k,m,n are positive, k=mn, and therefore y=2k=2mn.

The integers m and n are coprime. If a prime divided both, its square would divide both m^2=A and n^2=B, contradicting gcd(A,B)=1. Finally, x=m^2-n^2 is odd. The parity of an integer square equals the parity of the integer, so m and n must have opposite parity. This proves the required representation with all required restrictions.

Conversely, let m>n be coprime positive integers of opposite parity, and define
x=m^2-n^2, y=2mn, and z=m^2+n^2.
Then x,y,z are positive integers, and expansion gives
x^2+y^2=(m^2-n^2)^2+4m^2n^2=m^4+2m^2n^2+n^4=(m^2+n^2)^2=z^2.

It remains to prove primitivity. Because m and n have opposite parity, both x=m^2-n^2 and z=m^2+n^2 are odd. Suppose a prime p divided x,y,z. Then p is odd because x and z are odd. Since p divides x and z, it divides
z+x=2m^2
and
z-x=2n^2.
As p is odd, p divides m^2 and n^2, and hence p divides both m and n. This contradicts gcd(m,n)=1. Thus no prime divides x,y,z simultaneously. If gcd(x,y,z) were greater than 1, it would have a prime divisor common to x,y,z, so gcd(x,y,z)=1. Therefore the displayed formulas give a primitive Pythagorean triple.

## intuition
Normalize the even leg, split the difference of squares into two coprime half-factors whose product is a square, extract square roots, and check the converse by excluding a common prime.
