## statement
Let (x,y,z) be positive integers with

\[
x^2+y^2=z^2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\gcd(x,y,z)=1.
\]

Prove that, after possibly interchanging (x) and (y), there are coprime positive integers (m>n) of opposite parity such that

\[
x=m^2-n^2,\qquad y=2mn,\qquad z=m^2+n^2.
\]

Also prove the converse: every pair (m>n) with these properties gives a primitive Pythagorean triple by the displayed formulas.

## proof
We first prove the forward implication.

1. Pairwise coprimality. Suppose a prime p divides two members of x,y,z. If p divides x and y, then p divides z^2=x^2+y^2, so p divides z. If p divides x and z, then p divides y^2=z^2-x^2, so p divides y. If p divides y and z, then p divides x^2=z^2-y^2, so p divides x. Here we used the elementary prime-divisor property that a prime dividing an integer square divides the integer. In every case p would divide x,y,z, contrary to gcd(x,y,z)=1. Thus no prime divides any pair, and consequently gcd(x,y)=gcd(x,z)=gcd(y,z)=1.

2. Parity. The integers x and y cannot both be even, since then 2 would divide x,y,z (the equation would make z even), contrary to primitivity. They cannot both be odd: every odd square is congruent to 1 modulo 4, so x^2+y^2 would be congruent to 2 modulo 4, whereas every integer square is congruent to 0 or 1 modulo 4. Therefore exactly one of x,y is even. Interchange x and y if necessary so that y is even. Then x is odd. Also z is odd, because z^2=x^2+y^2 is odd.

3. Extraction of two coprime squares. Define
A=(z+x)/2 and B=(z-x)/2.
These are integers because x and z are odd. Since y>0, the equation gives z^2=x^2+y^2>x^2; as x,z are positive, z>x. Hence A and B are positive. Moreover
AB=((z+x)(z-x))/4=(z^2-x^2)/4=y^2/4=(y/2)^2.
If a positive integer d divides both A and B, then d divides A+B=z and A-B=x. Since gcd(x,z)=1 by Step 1, d=1. Hence gcd(A,B)=1.

We now justify that A and B are squares. In the prime factorization of AB=(y/2)^2, every prime has even exponent. Because gcd(A,B)=1, no prime occurs in both A and B. Thus the exponent of any prime occurring in A (respectively B) is exactly its exponent in AB and is even. Therefore there exist positive integers m,n such that A=m^2 and B=n^2.

Adding and subtracting the equations A=m^2 and B=n^2 yields
z=m^2+n^2 and x=m^2-n^2.
Also AB=m^2n^2=(y/2)^2. Since m,n,y are positive, taking the positive square roots gives y/2=mn, hence y=2mn. Because A>B (indeed A-B=x>0), we have m^2>n^2, and positivity gives m>n. If a prime divided both m and n, its square would divide both m^2=A and n^2=B, contradicting gcd(A,B)=1; hence gcd(m,n)=1. Therefore m and n cannot both be even. They also cannot both be odd, since then m^2-n^2=x would be even, contrary to the oddness of x. Thus m and n have opposite parity. This proves the required parametrization.

We next prove the converse. Let m>n be coprime positive integers of opposite parity, and define
x=m^2-n^2, y=2mn, z=m^2+n^2.
All three integers are positive. Direct expansion gives
x^2+y^2=(m^2-n^2)^2+(2mn)^2
=m^4-2m^2n^2+n^4+4m^2n^2
=m^4+2m^2n^2+n^4
=(m^2+n^2)^2=z^2.

It remains to prove primitivity. Because m,n have opposite parity, exactly one of m^2,n^2 is odd, so z=m^2+n^2 is odd. Suppose for contradiction that gcd(x,y,z)>1. Then some prime q divides x,y,z. Since z is odd, q is odd. From q dividing x and z, it follows that q divides
z+x=2m^2 and z-x=2n^2.
As q is an odd prime, q does not divide 2. Euclid's lemma applied to q dividing 2m^2 gives q dividing m^2 and hence q dividing m; similarly q divides n. This contradicts gcd(m,n)=1. Therefore no prime divides x,y,z simultaneously, so gcd(x,y,z)=1. The formulas therefore give a primitive Pythagorean triple, completing the converse.

## intuition
Normalize the even leg, factor y^2=(z-x)(z+x), divide both even factors by 2 to obtain coprime factors whose product is a square, and then recover the two square parameters. Opposite parity is exactly what makes the converse primitive.
