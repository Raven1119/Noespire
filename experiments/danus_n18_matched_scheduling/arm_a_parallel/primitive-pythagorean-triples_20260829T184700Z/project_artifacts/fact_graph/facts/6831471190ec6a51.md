## statement
Let (x,y,z) be positive integers with

\[
x^2+y^2=z^2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\gcd(x,y,z)=1.
\]

Prove that, after possibly interchanging (x) and (y), there are coprime positive integers (m>n) of opposite parity such that

\[
x=m^2-n^2,\qquad y=2mn,\qquad z=m^2+n^2.
\]

Also prove the converse: every pair (m>n) with these properties gives a primitive Pythagorean triple by the displayed formulas.

## proof
We first prove the forward implication.

We shall use the elementary fact that if a prime p divides an integer t^2, then p divides t. Indeed, in the prime factorization of t^2 every prime occurring has positive even exponent. We shall also use the following consequence: if two coprime positive integers A and B have square product, then each is a square. To prove this, write their prime factorizations. Coprimality means that every prime occurs in at most one of A and B. Since every prime exponent in the square AB is even, every prime exponent in A and every prime exponent in B is even.

The three integers x,y,z are pairwise coprime. For example, if a prime p divided x and y, then p would divide z^2=x^2+y^2 and hence z, contradicting gcd(x,y,z)=1. The same argument, using y^2=z^2-x^2 and x^2=z^2-y^2, proves gcd(x,z)=gcd(y,z)=1.

The legs x and y cannot both be even by pairwise coprimality. They cannot both be odd, because an odd square is congruent to 1 modulo 4, so x^2+y^2 would be congruent to 2 modulo 4, whereas a square is congruent only to 0 or 1 modulo 4. Therefore exactly one leg is even. After possibly interchanging x and y, assume that y is even. Then x is odd, and z is odd because z^2=x^2+y^2 is odd.

Define
A=(z+x)/2 and B=(z-x)/2.
These are integers. Since y>0, the equation gives z^2>x^2, and positivity of z and x gives z>x; hence A and B are positive. Moreover,
AB=((z+x)(z-x))/4=(z^2-x^2)/4=y^2/4=(y/2)^2.
If a positive integer d divides both A and B, then d divides A+B=z and A-B=x. Since gcd(x,z)=1, this gives d=1. Thus gcd(A,B)=1.

By the coprime-factor lemma proved above, there are positive integers m and n such that A=m^2 and B=n^2. Since A-B=x>0, we have A>B and therefore m>n. Adding and subtracting the equations for A and B gives
z=A+B=m^2+n^2
and
x=A-B=m^2-n^2.
Also,
(y/2)^2=AB=m^2n^2=(mn)^2.
Both y/2 and mn are positive integers, so y/2=mn and hence y=2mn.

The integers m and n are coprime: if a positive integer d divided both, then d^2 would divide both m^2=A and n^2=B, whereas gcd(A,B)=1, forcing d=1. They cannot both be even because they are coprime. If they were both odd, then m^2-n^2 and 2mn would both be even, so x and y would have common divisor 2, contradicting gcd(x,y)=1. Consequently m and n have opposite parity. This completes the forward implication.

For the converse, let m>n be coprime positive integers of opposite parity, and define
x=m^2-n^2, y=2mn, and z=m^2+n^2.
All three are positive. Direct expansion gives
x^2+y^2=(m^2-n^2)^2+(2mn)^2
=m^4-2m^2n^2+n^4+4m^2n^2
=(m^2+n^2)^2=z^2.

It remains to prove primitivity. Because m and n have opposite parity, m^2-n^2 and m^2+n^2 are odd, so x and z are odd. Suppose that a prime p divided x,y,z. Then p is not 2 because x and z are odd, so p is odd. Since p divides x and z, it divides
z+x=2m^2 and z-x=2n^2.
As p is odd, p divides m^2 and n^2, and hence p divides both m and n. This contradicts gcd(m,n)=1. Thus no prime divides x,y,z simultaneously. If gcd(x,y,z) were greater than 1, it would have a prime divisor common to all three; therefore gcd(x,y,z)=1. The resulting triple is primitive, as required.

## intuition
Factor the difference of the two odd squares z^2-x^2 into the coprime integers (z+x)/2 and (z-x)/2. Their product is the square (y/2)^2, so each factor is a square.
