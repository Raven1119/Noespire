## statement
Let (x,y,z) be positive integers with

\[
x^2+y^2=z^2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\gcd(x,y,z)=1.
\]

Prove that, after possibly interchanging (x) and (y), there are coprime positive integers (m>n) of opposite parity such that

\[
x=m^2-n^2,\qquad y=2mn,\qquad z=m^2+n^2.
\]

Also prove the converse: every pair (m>n) with these properties gives a primitive Pythagorean triple by the displayed formulas.

## proof
We first prove the forward implication.

1. Pairwise coprimality. We claim that each pair among x,y,z is coprime. For example, if a prime p divided both x and y, then p would divide z^2=x^2+y^2, and therefore p would divide z, contradicting gcd(x,y,z)=1. If p divided x and z, then p would divide y^2=z^2-x^2 and hence y, with the same contradiction. The case of y and z is identical after exchanging x and y. Thus gcd(x,y)=gcd(x,z)=gcd(y,z)=1.

2. Parity. Every integer square is congruent to 0 or 1 modulo 4. The integers x and y cannot both be odd, because then x^2+y^2 would be congruent to 2 modulo 4, whereas z^2 cannot be congruent to 2 modulo 4. They cannot both be even, because then gcd(x,y,z) would be at least 2 (the equation would also make z even). Therefore exactly one of x,y is even. Interchange x and y if necessary so that x is odd and y is even. Reducing x^2+y^2=z^2 modulo 2 then shows that z is odd.

3. Coprime factorization. Define
A=(z+x)/2 and B=(z-x)/2.
Because z and x are odd, A and B are integers. Also z>x>0: indeed z^2=x^2+y^2>x^2 and z,x are positive. Hence A and B are positive and A>B. Moreover,
AB=((z+x)(z-x))/4=(z^2-x^2)/4=y^2/4=(y/2)^2.
If a positive integer d divides both A and B, then d divides A+B=z and A-B=x. Since gcd(x,z)=1 by Step 1, d=1. Thus gcd(A,B)=1.

We now justify in full the square-factor step. Consider the unique prime factorizations of A and B. Since gcd(A,B)=1, no prime has positive exponent in both factorizations. Since AB=(y/2)^2 is a square, the exponent of every prime in the factorization of AB is even. For each prime occurring in A, its exponent in AB equals its exponent in A, and hence is even; the same argument applies to B. Thus every prime exponent in each of A and B is even. Consequently there are positive integers m,n such that A=m^2 and B=n^2.

Since A>B, we have m^2>n^2 and therefore m>n. Adding and subtracting the identities
z+x=2m^2 and z-x=2n^2
gives
z=m^2+n^2 and x=m^2-n^2.
Also (y/2)^2=AB=m^2n^2. Both y/2 and mn are positive, so equality of their squares gives y/2=mn, and hence y=2mn.

If a positive integer d divides both m and n, then d^2 divides both m^2=A and n^2=B; because gcd(A,B)=1, this forces d=1. Hence gcd(m,n)=1. Finally m and n cannot both be even because they are coprime. They cannot both be odd either, because then x=m^2-n^2 would be even, whereas x was chosen odd. Therefore m and n have opposite parity. This proves the required parametrization.

For the converse, let m>n be coprime positive integers of opposite parity and define
x=m^2-n^2, y=2mn, and z=m^2+n^2.
These are positive integers, and direct expansion gives
x^2+y^2=(m^2-n^2)^2+(2mn)^2
=m^4-2m^2n^2+n^4+4m^2n^2
=m^4+2m^2n^2+n^4
=(m^2+n^2)^2=z^2.

It remains to prove primitivity. Suppose, for contradiction, that gcd(x,y,z)>1, and choose a prime p dividing gcd(x,y,z). If p=2, this is impossible because opposite parity of m,n makes m^2+n^2=z odd. Thus p is odd. Since p divides both x and z, it divides
z+x=2m^2 and z-x=2n^2.
Because p is odd, p divides m^2 and n^2, so primality of p implies p divides both m and n. This contradicts gcd(m,n)=1. Hence no prime divides gcd(x,y,z), so gcd(x,y,z)=1. The displayed formulas therefore produce a primitive Pythagorean triple, completing the converse.

## intuition
Split the difference of squares z^2-x^2 into coprime half-sum and half-difference factors. Their product is a square, so coprimality forces each factor to be a square.
