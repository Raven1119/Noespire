## statement
Let (x,y,z) be positive integers with

\[
x^2+y^2=z^2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\gcd(x,y,z)=1.
\]

Prove that, after possibly interchanging (x) and (y), there are coprime positive integers (m>n) of opposite parity such that

\[
x=m^2-n^2,\qquad y=2mn,\qquad z=m^2+n^2.
\]

Also prove the converse: every pair (m>n) with these properties gives a primitive Pythagorean triple by the displayed formulas.

## proof
We first prove the forward implication.

A preliminary consequence of the hypotheses is that x, y, and z are pairwise coprime. Indeed, if a prime p divided any two of x,y,z, then the equation x^2+y^2=z^2 would imply that p divides the square of the remaining integer. A prime which divides an integer square divides the integer, so p would divide all three of x,y,z, contrary to gcd(x,y,z)=1.

For every integer a, its square is congruent modulo 4 to 0 if a is even and to 1 if a is odd. The integers x and y cannot both be even because they are pairwise coprime. They also cannot both be odd, because then x^2+y^2 would be congruent to 2 modulo 4, whereas z^2 must be congruent to 0 or 1 modulo 4. Hence exactly one of x and y is even. After interchanging x and y if necessary, assume that y is even. Then x is odd. Moreover z is odd, since z^2=x^2+y^2 is odd.

Because x>0 and z,y>0, the equation gives z^2>y^2 and therefore z>y. Factor the equation as
(z-y)(z+y)=x^2.
Both z-y and z+y are positive odd integers. They are coprime: if a prime p divided both, then p would divide their sum 2z and their difference 2y. Since p divides the odd integer z-y, p is odd, so p does not divide 2. Consequently p would divide both z and y, contradicting their pairwise coprimality.

We next justify the square decomposition. In the unique prime factorizations of the coprime positive integers z-y and z+y, each prime occurs in at most one of the two factors. Its exponent in their product is therefore its exponent in that one factor. Since the product equals the square x^2, every such exponent is even. Thus each factor is a perfect square. There are positive integers r and s such that
z+y=r^2 and z-y=s^2.
The two factors are odd, so r and s are odd. Also z+y>z-y>0, hence r>s>0. Their squares are coprime, so r and s are coprime.

Define
m=(r+s)/2 and n=(r-s)/2.
Because r and s are odd, m and n are integers. Since r>s>0, we have m>n>0. If a prime p divided both m and n, it would divide m+n=r and m-n=s, contradicting gcd(r,s)=1; hence gcd(m,n)=1. Further, m+n=r is odd, so m and n have opposite parity.

The definitions give r=m+n and s=m-n. Adding and subtracting the two square equations now gives
z=(r^2+s^2)/2=m^2+n^2
and
y=(r^2-s^2)/2=2mn.
Finally, (z-y)(z+y)=x^2 gives r^2s^2=x^2. All of x,r,s are positive, so x=rs=(m+n)(m-n)=m^2-n^2. This proves the required parametrization.

For the converse, let m>n be coprime positive integers of opposite parity, and define
x=m^2-n^2, y=2mn, and z=m^2+n^2.
Then x,y,z are positive integers, and direct expansion gives
x^2+y^2=(m^2-n^2)^2+(2mn)^2
=m^4+2m^2n^2+n^4
=(m^2+n^2)^2=z^2.

It remains to prove primitivity. Because m and n have opposite parity, m^2+n^2 is odd, so 2 does not divide z. Suppose that a prime p divided x,y,z. Then p would divide
z+x=2m^2 and z-x=2n^2.
The prime p cannot be 2 because z is odd. Thus p is odd, so p divides both m^2 and n^2, and hence p divides both m and n. This contradicts gcd(m,n)=1. Therefore no prime divides x,y,z simultaneously. Since x,y,z are positive, this is equivalent to gcd(x,y,z)=1. Hence the displayed formulas give a primitive Pythagorean triple, completing the converse and the proof.
