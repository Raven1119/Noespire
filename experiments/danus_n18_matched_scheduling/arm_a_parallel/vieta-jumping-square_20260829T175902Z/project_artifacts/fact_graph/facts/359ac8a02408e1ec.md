## statement
Let (a) and (b) be positive integers. Suppose that

\[
\frac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}
\]

is an integer. Prove that this integer is a perfect square.

## proof
Let k=(a^2+b^2)/(ab+1). By the hypothesis and positivity of a and b, k is a positive integer, and the defining equality is

(1)  a^2+b^2=k(ab+1).

Suppose, for contradiction, that the conclusion is false. Then there exists at least one ordered pair (a,b) of positive integers satisfying (1) for which k is not a perfect square. Among all such ordered pairs, choose one for which a+b is minimal. Equation (1) and the value of k are unchanged when a and b are interchanged, so after interchanging them if necessary we may assume a>=b.

If a=b, then k=2a^2/(a^2+1)<2. Since k is a positive integer, this forces k=1, which is a perfect square, contrary to the choice of the pair. Therefore a>b.

Define the integer c=kb-a. Rearranging (1) gives

(2)  a(a-kb)=k-b^2,

and, because c=kb-a, equivalently

(3)  ac=b^2-k.

We claim that c>0. Suppose instead that c<=0, so a>=kb. If a=kb, then (2) gives k=b^2, which makes k a perfect square, a contradiction. Hence a>kb. Both a and kb are integers, so a-kb>=1. Also b>=1, hence a>kb>=k. Consequently

a(a-kb)>=a>k.

But (2) says that this same quantity equals k-b^2, and b>=1 gives k-b^2<k. This is impossible. Thus c>0.

It now follows from (3) that

0<c=(b^2-k)/a<b^2/a<b,

where the last strict inequality uses a>b>0. We next verify directly that (b,c) satisfies the same equation with the same k. Using c-kb=-a and (3),

c^2+b^2-k(bc+1)=c(c-kb)+b^2-k=-ac+b^2-k=0.

Therefore c^2+b^2=k(bc+1), so (b,c) is another ordered pair of positive integers whose quotient is the same nonsquare integer k. Since c<b<a, we have b+c<a+b. This contradicts the minimal choice of (a,b).

The contradiction proves that no nonsquare integer can occur as the given quotient. Therefore the integer in the statement is a perfect square.

## intuition
A nonsquare quotient would admit a minimal positive solution. Viewing the equation as a quadratic in its larger entry produces the other integral root c=kb-a; nonsquareness forces 0<c<b, contradicting minimality.
