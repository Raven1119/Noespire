## statement
Let (a) and (b) be positive integers. Suppose that

\[
\frac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}
\]

is an integer. Prove that this integer is a perfect square.

## proof
Let k=(a^2+b^2)/(ab+1). Since a and b are positive integers, both a^2+b^2 and ab+1 are positive, so the assumed integer k is a positive integer. Equivalently,

(1)  a^2+b^2=k(ab+1).

Keep k fixed. The set of ordered pairs of positive integers satisfying (1) is nonempty because it contains the pair from the hypothesis. By the well-ordering principle, choose from this set a pair whose coordinate sum is minimal. Since (1) is symmetric in the two coordinates, interchange the coordinates if necessary and denote the chosen pair again by (a,b), with a>=b.

Define c=kb-a, which is an integer. We first prove c>=0. Suppose instead that c<0, and define d=-c=a-kb. Then d is a positive integer and a=kb+d. Substituting this expression for a into (1) gives

(kb+d)^2+b^2=k((kb+d)b+1).

Expanding both sides and cancelling k^2b^2 gives

2kbd+d^2+b^2=kbd+k,

and hence

(2)  kbd+d^2+b^2=k.

Because k,b,d are positive integers, b d>=1 and therefore kbd>=k. Thus the left side of (2) is strictly greater than k because d^2+b^2>0, contradicting (2). Consequently c>=0.

Rearranging (1) yields

(3)  ac=a(kb-a)=kab-a^2=b^2-k.

Using a+c=kb and (3), we compute

c^2+b^2=c^2+ac+k=c(a+c)+k=ckb+k=k(cb+1).

Therefore (c,b) satisfies the same equation with the same fixed positive integer k whenever c is positive.

Assume c>0. From (3) and k>0 we obtain ac=b^2-k<b^2. Since a>=b>0, division by a gives

c<b^2/a<=b.

In particular c<a, so c+b<a+b. Thus (c,b) is a pair of positive integers satisfying (1) whose coordinate sum is smaller than the minimal chosen sum a+b, a contradiction. Hence c cannot be positive. Since c>=0, it follows that c=0. Equation (3) then gives 0=b^2-k, so k=b^2. Therefore the integer (a^2+b^2)/(ab+1), namely k, is a perfect square.

## intuition
Fix the quotient and choose a solution of least coordinate sum. Replacing the larger coordinate a by its Vieta conjugate kb-a preserves the equation. Algebra rules out a negative conjugate; a positive conjugate is smaller than b and contradicts minimality. Hence it is zero, forcing k=b^2.
