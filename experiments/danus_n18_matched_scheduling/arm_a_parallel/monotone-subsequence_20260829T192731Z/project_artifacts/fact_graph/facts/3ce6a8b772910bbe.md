## statement
Let (n) be a positive integer. Prove that every sequence of (n^2+1) distinct real numbers contains either a strictly increasing subsequence of length (n+1) or a strictly decreasing subsequence of length (n+1).

## proof
Fix a positive integer n and an arbitrary sequence x_1,x_2,...,x_{n^2+1} of distinct real numbers. A subsequence means a selection of terms whose indices remain in their original strictly increasing order.

For every index i with 1<=i<=n^2+1, define p_i to be the maximum integer r for which there exist indices 1<=i_1<i_2<...<i_r=i satisfying x_{i_1}<x_{i_2}<...<x_{i_r}. Define q_i to be the maximum integer s for which there exist indices 1<=j_1<j_2<...<j_s=i satisfying x_{j_1}>x_{j_2}>...>x_{j_s}. These maxima exist: the one-term subsequence x_i is admissible in both definitions, and every subsequence ending at i has length at most i. Thus p_i and q_i are positive integers.

If p_i>=n+1 for some i, the subsequence occurring in the definition of p_i has a first n+1 terms that form a strictly increasing subsequence of length n+1. Likewise, if q_i>=n+1 for some i, the definition of q_i supplies a strictly decreasing subsequence of length n+1. It therefore remains only to rule out the simultaneous inequalities p_i<=n and q_i<=n for every i.

Assume for contradiction that p_i<=n and q_i<=n for every i. Then every ordered pair (p_i,q_i) belongs to {1,2,...,n} x {1,2,...,n}. We prove that the n^2+1 such ordered pairs are pairwise distinct. Take any indices i<j. Since x_i and x_j are distinct real numbers, exactly one of x_i<x_j and x_i>x_j holds. If x_i<x_j, choose indices witnessing a strictly increasing subsequence of length p_i ending at x_i. Appending the later term x_j gives a strictly increasing subsequence of length p_i+1 ending at x_j, so p_j>=p_i+1 and hence p_j is not equal to p_i. If x_i>x_j, choose indices witnessing a strictly decreasing subsequence of length q_i ending at x_i. Appending x_j gives a strictly decreasing subsequence of length q_i+1 ending at x_j, so q_j>=q_i+1 and hence q_j is not equal to q_i. In either case, (p_i,q_i) is not equal to (p_j,q_j). Because any two different indices can be placed in increasing order, all n^2+1 ordered pairs are distinct.

However, {1,2,...,n} x {1,2,...,n} contains exactly n*n=n^2 ordered pairs: there are n choices for the first coordinate and, for each such choice, n choices for the second coordinate. It cannot contain n^2+1 distinct ordered pairs. This contradiction shows that some p_i>=n+1 or some q_i>=n+1, and the corresponding subsequence has the required length and monotonicity.

## intuition
Label each sequence position by the lengths of the longest increasing and decreasing subsequences ending there. Any earlier-later pair forces at least one coordinate to increase, so the labels are all distinct. If both kinds of subsequence had length at most n, only n^2 labels would be available for n^2+1 positions.
