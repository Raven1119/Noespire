## statement
Let (n) be a positive integer. Prove that every sequence of (n^2+1) distinct real numbers contains either a strictly increasing subsequence of length (n+1) or a strictly decreasing subsequence of length (n+1).

## proof
Write the given sequence in its original order as x_1,x_2,...,x_{n^2+1}. For every index i in {1,2,...,n^2+1}, define p_i to be the greatest possible length of a strictly increasing subsequence whose last term is x_i, and define q_i to be the greatest possible length of a strictly decreasing subsequence whose last term is x_i. These greatest lengths exist because the sequence is finite and the one-term subsequence consisting of x_i is both strictly increasing and strictly decreasing.

Assume for contradiction that the sequence has neither a strictly increasing subsequence of length n+1 nor a strictly decreasing subsequence of length n+1. Then 1<=p_i<=n and 1<=q_i<=n for every i, so every ordered pair (p_i,q_i) belongs to {1,2,...,n} x {1,2,...,n}.

We prove that these ordered pairs are pairwise distinct. Let i and j satisfy 1<=i<j<=n^2+1. Because all terms of the original sequence are distinct, either x_i<x_j or x_i>x_j.

If x_i<x_j, choose a strictly increasing subsequence of length p_i ending at x_i; such a subsequence exists by the definition of p_i. Appending x_j to it is valid because j>i, and the appended value is larger than x_i. The resulting subsequence is strictly increasing, ends at x_j, and has length p_i+1. Therefore p_j>=p_i+1, so p_j is not equal to p_i.

If x_i>x_j, choose a strictly decreasing subsequence of length q_i ending at x_i. Appending x_j is valid because j>i, and the appended value is smaller than x_i. The resulting subsequence is strictly decreasing, ends at x_j, and has length q_i+1. Therefore q_j>=q_i+1, so q_j is not equal to q_i.

Thus (p_i,q_i) is not equal to (p_j,q_j) whenever i<j. Hence the n^2+1 indices give n^2+1 distinct ordered pairs in {1,2,...,n} x {1,2,...,n}. But that Cartesian product has exactly n*n=n^2 elements, which is impossible. This contradiction proves that at least one of the two required subsequences exists.

## intuition
Assign to each term the two longest monotone-subsequence lengths ending there. Earlier distinct terms force at least one coordinate to increase, so the assigned pairs are distinct; avoiding length n+1 leaves only n^2 possible pairs.
